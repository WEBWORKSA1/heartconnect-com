Blood Pressure Numbers Explained: Normal, Elevated and High

What your systolic and diastolic numbers mean, the adult blood-pressure categories, how to measure accurately at home, and proven ways to lower high blood pressure.

✍️ HeartConnect Editorial Team📅 Updated 2026-11-18⏱ 8 min read📚 Based on guidance from leading heart organisations

High blood pressure (hypertension) is often called a "silent killer" because it usually causes no symptoms while it quietly damages arteries, the heart, the brain, the kidneys and the eyes. It is one of the most important — and most treatable — risk factors for heart attack and stroke. The only way to know your blood pressure is to measure it.

What the two numbers mean

  • Systolic (top number) — the pressure in your arteries when your heart beats.
  • Diastolic (bottom number) — the pressure in your arteries when your heart rests between beats.

Blood pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) and written as, for example, 118/76 mmHg.

Adult blood pressure categories

Many clinicians use the following categories from the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association. Other countries may use slightly different thresholds (for example, some define hypertension as 140/90 or higher), so follow your clinician's advice.

A reading above 180/120 with symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness, weakness, vision change or difficulty speaking is an emergency — call your local emergency number.

How to measure accurately at home

  1. Use a validated upper-arm cuff monitor in the correct cuff size.
  2. Avoid caffeine, exercise and smoking for 30 minutes beforehand, and empty your bladder.
  3. Sit quietly for 5 minutes with your back supported, feet flat and arm resting at heart level.
  4. Don't talk during the measurement. Take two readings one minute apart and record both.
  5. Measure at the same times each day — for example, morning and evening — for a week before an appointment.

Keep a log with our free BP tracker; it charts your readings and exports a CSV you can share with your doctor.

What raises blood pressure?

Common contributors include high salt (sodium) intake, low potassium intake, excess weight, physical inactivity, heavy alcohol use, smoking, poor sleep or sleep apnoea, chronic stress, family history and ageing. Some medicines and conditions, such as kidney disease or thyroid problems, can also raise it.

Proven ways to lower blood pressure

  • Cut sodium — cook more at home, read labels and limit processed, restaurant and salty snack foods.
  • Follow a DASH-style eating pattern — plenty of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, low-fat dairy, legumes and nuts. See our heart-healthy diet guide.
  • Move regularly — aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week.
  • Reach a healthier weight — even modest weight loss can lower readings.
  • Limit alcohol and stop smoking.
  • Sleep 7–9 hours and ask about sleep apnoea if you snore loudly.
  • Take medicines as prescribed. Many people need medication in addition to lifestyle changes — this is normal and effective.

The bottom line

Know your numbers, measure correctly, and work with your clinician on a plan. Controlling blood pressure is one of the most powerful things you can do to protect your heart and brain.

Medical note: This article is general education, not a diagnosis. Talk with a qualified clinician about your own situation. If you think someone is having a heart attack or stroke, call emergency services immediately.
