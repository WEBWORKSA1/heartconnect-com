Exercise for Heart Health: How Much, What Kind and How to Start Safely

The weekly activity targets for adults, the best types of exercise for your heart, how to use heart-rate zones, and safe ways to start.

✍️ HeartConnect Editorial Team📅 Updated 2026-11-18⏱ 7 min read📚 Based on guidance from leading heart organisations

Regular physical activity is one of the most effective things you can do for your heart. It lowers blood pressure, improves cholesterol and blood sugar, helps manage weight, reduces stress and improves sleep. The good news: you don't need a gym or marathon training to benefit.

How much is enough?

The World Health Organization recommends that adults do at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent mix — plus muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days a week. Older adults should also include balance training.

Any amount is better than none. If 150 minutes feels impossible, start with 10 minutes a day and build up.

Moderate vs. vigorous: the talk test

  • Moderate: you can talk but not sing — brisk walking, cycling on flat ground, gardening, dancing, water aerobics.
  • Vigorous: you can only say a few words before pausing for breath — jogging, running, fast cycling, swimming laps, skipping, sports like football or badminton.

You can also use heart-rate zones. Find yours with our target heart-rate calculator.

The best types of exercise for your heart

  • Aerobic (cardio): the foundation for heart health — walking, cycling, swimming, dancing.
  • Strength training: body-weight exercises, resistance bands or weights twice a week help blood sugar, metabolism and everyday function.
  • Flexibility and balance: yoga, tai chi and stretching reduce falls and help you stay active.
  • Less sitting: break up long periods of sitting with 2–3 minutes of movement every 30 minutes.

Getting started safely

  1. If you have heart disease, chest symptoms, diabetes, high blood pressure or have been inactive for a long time, check with your clinician first.
  2. Warm up for 5 minutes and cool down for 5 minutes.
  3. Increase time or intensity gradually — about 10% per week.
  4. Stay hydrated and avoid exercising hard in extreme heat or cold.
  5. Choose activities you enjoy; consistency beats intensity.
Stop and get help if you feel chest pain or pressure, unusual shortness of breath, dizziness, fainting or a racing or irregular heartbeat during exercise.

After a heart event: cardiac rehabilitation

If you have had a heart attack, stent, bypass surgery, valve surgery or heart failure, ask about a cardiac rehabilitation program. These supervised programs combine safe exercise, education and support and are linked to better recovery and fewer future problems. Our Heart Care Concierge can help you find one.
