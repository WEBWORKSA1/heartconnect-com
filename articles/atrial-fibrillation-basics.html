Atrial Fibrillation (AFib): Symptoms, Stroke Risk and Treatment Options

Understand atrial fibrillation — what it is, common symptoms, why it raises stroke risk, how it's diagnosed and the main treatment options.

✍️ HeartConnect Editorial Team📅 Updated 2026-11-18⏱ 7 min read📚 Based on guidance from leading heart organisations

Atrial fibrillation (AFib or AF) is the most common type of irregular heartbeat (arrhythmia). In AFib, the heart's upper chambers (atria) quiver chaotically instead of contracting in a steady rhythm. This can make the heartbeat irregular and often fast, and — importantly — allows blood to pool and form clots that can travel to the brain and cause a stroke.

Common symptoms

  • Palpitations: a fluttering, pounding or irregular heartbeat
  • Tiredness or reduced ability to exercise
  • Shortness of breath
  • Dizziness or light-headedness
  • Chest discomfort

Many people have no symptoms at all. AFib is sometimes first found during a routine pulse check, an ECG, or by a smartwatch alert.

Seek emergency care for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or any stroke signs — face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty.

Types of AFib

  • Paroxysmal: comes and goes, usually stopping on its own within 7 days.
  • Persistent: lasts longer than 7 days and may need treatment to restore normal rhythm.
  • Long-standing persistent and permanent: continuous AFib where the focus is usually on controlling heart rate and preventing stroke.

Who is at risk?

Risk rises with age and with high blood pressure, heart failure, coronary or valve disease, diabetes, obesity, sleep apnoea, thyroid disease, heavy alcohol use and a family history of AFib.

How AFib is diagnosed

An electrocardiogram (ECG) confirms AFib. Because it can come and go, your doctor may use a wearable monitor (Holter or event monitor) for days or weeks. An echocardiogram checks heart structure, and blood tests look for causes such as thyroid problems. See our guide to heart tests.

Treatment goals and options

  1. Prevent stroke: many people with AFib need blood thinners (anticoagulants). Your doctor estimates your stroke and bleeding risk to decide. For some people who cannot take long-term blood thinners, a procedure to close the left atrial appendage may be an option.
  2. Control heart rate: medicines such as beta-blockers or calcium-channel blockers slow the heartbeat.
  3. Control rhythm: anti-arrhythmic medicines, electrical cardioversion, or catheter ablation, which scars small areas of heart tissue that trigger AFib.
  4. Treat risk factors: controlling blood pressure, losing excess weight, treating sleep apnoea, limiting alcohol and staying active can reduce AFib episodes.

Living well with AFib

  • Take blood thinners exactly as prescribed and tell every clinician and dentist you take them.
  • Learn to check your pulse and keep a symptom diary.
  • Limit alcohol and be cautious with stimulants.
  • Ask about specialist care from an electrophysiologist (a heart-rhythm specialist).
