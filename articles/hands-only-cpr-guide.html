Hands-Only CPR: A Simple Guide That Can Save a Life

Learn how to recognise cardiac arrest and perform Hands-Only CPR in two steps, how to use an AED, and why bystander action doubles survival chances.

✍️ HeartConnect Editorial Team📅 Updated 2026-11-18⏱ 6 min read📚 Based on guidance from leading heart organisations

Sudden cardiac arrest happens when the heart suddenly stops pumping blood effectively, usually because of a dangerous heart rhythm. The person collapses, becomes unresponsive and stops breathing normally. Without immediate help, death can occur within minutes. Starting CPR right away can double or even triple the chance of survival.

Hands-Only CPR is CPR without mouth-to-mouth breaths. It's recommended for untrained bystanders helping a teen or adult who suddenly collapses.

Recognise cardiac arrest

  • The person suddenly collapses.
  • They don't respond when you tap their shoulders and shout.
  • They are not breathing, or are only gasping.

Hands-Only CPR in two steps

  1. Call emergency services (or ask someone else to) and put the phone on speaker. Send someone to find an AED.
  2. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest:
    • Kneel beside the person. Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest, other hand on top, fingers interlocked.
    • Keep your arms straight, shoulders over your hands.
    • Push down about 5–6 cm (2–2.4 inches) for an adult.
    • Push at a rate of 100–120 compressions per minute — about the beat of many popular dance songs.
    • Let the chest come back up fully between pushes.
    • Don't stop until help arrives, an AED is ready, or the person starts breathing normally. Swap with another person every 2 minutes if you can.

Using an AED

An automated external defibrillator (AED) is a portable device that can shock the heart back into a normal rhythm. Anyone can use one:

  1. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts.
  2. Attach the pads to the bare chest as shown in the pictures on the pads.
  3. Make sure nobody is touching the person when the AED analyses or shocks.
  4. Restart compressions immediately after a shock, or if no shock is advised.

Common worries

  • "What if I hurt them?" Without CPR, a person in cardiac arrest will die. Doing something is far better than doing nothing.
  • "I'm not trained." The dispatcher can coach you. Hands-Only CPR is easy to learn.
  • Children and infants: conventional CPR with breaths is recommended for infants and children, and for drowning or drug-overdose victims, if you are trained. If not, chest compressions are still better than nothing.

Take the next step

Watch a short CPR demonstration in our video hub, and consider a hands-on course with a recognised training provider in your area. Organise an awareness drive at your school or workplace through our community — it's one of our monthly contests.

Medical note: This article is general education, not a diagnosis. Talk with a qualified clinician about your own situation. If you think someone is having a heart attack or stroke, call emergency services immediately.
