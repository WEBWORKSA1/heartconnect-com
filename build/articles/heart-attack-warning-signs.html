A heart attack happens when blood flow to part of the heart muscle is blocked, usually by a blood clot forming on a narrowed, plaque-filled coronary artery. Without oxygen-rich blood, heart muscle starts to die. The faster blood flow is restored, the more heart muscle can be saved — which is why recognising the signs and acting immediately matters more than anything else in this article.

If you think you or someone else is having a heart attack, call your local emergency number now. Do not drive yourself to hospital. Emergency teams can begin treatment on the way.

The most common warning signs

  • Chest discomfort — pressure, squeezing, fullness, heaviness or pain in the centre or left side of the chest. It may last more than a few minutes, or go away and come back.
  • Discomfort in other areas of the upper body — one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw or upper stomach.
  • Shortness of breath — with or without chest discomfort.
  • Other signs — breaking out in a cold sweat, nausea or vomiting, light-headedness or sudden unusual tiredness.

Heart attacks are not always dramatic. Many start slowly with mild discomfort, and people often wait too long because they aren't sure what is happening. If in doubt, get checked.

Signs can be different in women, older adults and people with diabetes

Chest pain is still the most common symptom for women, but women are more likely than men to have other symptoms such as shortness of breath, nausea or vomiting, back or jaw pain, and extreme fatigue. Older adults and people with diabetes may have fewer or milder symptoms — sometimes called a "silent" heart attack — because nerve damage or age can change how pain is felt. These differences are one reason heart attacks are sometimes missed or delayed.

What to do, step by step

  1. Call emergency services immediately. Say you think it may be a heart attack.
  2. Stop all activity and sit or lie in a comfortable position.
  3. Aspirin: the dispatcher may advise chewing a regular adult aspirin if the person isn't allergic and has no reason to avoid it. Follow their instructions.
  4. Unlock the door and, if possible, ask someone to meet the ambulance.
  5. If the person collapses and isn't breathing normally, start Hands-Only CPR and ask someone to find an AED (automated external defibrillator).

Heart attack vs. cardiac arrest

What it isA "circulation" problem: a blocked artery starves heart muscle of oxygen.An "electrical" problem: the heart suddenly stops pumping effectively.
What you seeThe person is usually awake, with symptoms that may build over minutes or hours.The person collapses, is unresponsive and isn't breathing normally.
What to doCall emergency services.Call emergency services, start CPR, use an AED.

A heart attack can trigger cardiac arrest, so be ready to start CPR.

Why time matters

Hospitals can reopen blocked arteries with clot-dissolving medicines or with angioplasty and stents. These treatments work best when given early. Calling emergency services also means trained staff can monitor heart rhythm and treat dangerous rhythms on the way.

Lower your risk of a heart attack

  • Know and manage your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar.
  • Don't smoke or vape, and avoid second-hand smoke.
  • Be active for at least 150 minutes a week (see our exercise guide).
  • Eat a heart-healthy diet rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes and nuts.
  • Take prescribed medicines as directed and keep follow-up appointments.

Share these warning signs with family and colleagues. A few minutes of awareness today could save a life tomorrow.
