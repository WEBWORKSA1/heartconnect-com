Cholesterol is a waxy substance your body needs to build cells and make hormones. Your liver makes most of it; the rest comes from food. Problems start when too much of certain types builds up in artery walls, forming plaque that narrows arteries and can rupture to cause a heart attack or stroke.

The parts of a cholesterol test (lipid panel)

  • LDL cholesterol ("bad") — the main driver of plaque build-up. Lower is generally better.
  • HDL cholesterol ("good") — helps carry cholesterol away from arteries. Higher levels are associated with lower risk.
  • Triglycerides — a type of fat in the blood; high levels are linked to heart disease and are common with excess weight, high sugar and alcohol intake, and diabetes.
  • Total cholesterol — overall amount.
  • Non-HDL cholesterol — total minus HDL; captures all the potentially harmful particles.

General reference ranges for adults

MeasureDesirable (general guide)
Total cholesterolLess than 200 mg/dL (about 5.2 mmol/L)
LDL cholesterolLess than 100 mg/dL (about 2.6 mmol/L); lower targets for people with heart disease or high risk
HDL cholesterol40 mg/dL or higher for men, 50 mg/dL or higher for women
TriglyceridesLess than 150 mg/dL (about 1.7 mmol/L)

Your personal target depends on your overall risk — age, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, family history and whether you already have heart disease. Your clinician may set a much lower LDL goal. Try our cholesterol ratio calculator.

Familial hypercholesterolaemia: when it runs in the family

Some people inherit a condition that causes very high LDL from birth. Clues include LDL above about 190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L), early heart disease in close relatives, or cholesterol deposits around the eyes or tendons. It is very treatable, and family members should be tested.

How to improve your numbers

  • Swap saturated and trans fats (fatty processed meats, butter, ghee in large amounts, palm oil, fried and baked snacks) for unsaturated fats such as olive, canola, mustard or sunflower oils, nuts and seeds.
  • Eat more soluble fibre — oats, barley, beans, lentils, apples and citrus fruit help lower LDL.
  • Cut added sugar and refined carbs to lower triglycerides.
  • Be active — exercise raises HDL and lowers triglycerides.
  • Limit alcohol, which can raise triglycerides.
  • Don't smoke — smoking lowers HDL and damages arteries.
  • Medicines — statins and other cholesterol-lowering drugs are well studied and substantially reduce heart attacks and strokes in people at higher risk. Discuss benefits and side effects with your clinician.

How often to get tested

Many guidelines suggest adults have a lipid test every 4–6 years if risk is low, and more often if levels are high, you have other risk factors or you are on treatment. Children with a strong family history may need earlier testing.
