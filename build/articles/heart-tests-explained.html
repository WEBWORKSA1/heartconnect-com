If your doctor suspects a heart problem, you may be offered one or more tests. Knowing what each test does can make the process less stressful and help you ask better questions.

Blood tests

Common blood tests include a lipid profile (cholesterol and triglycerides), HbA1c or fasting glucose (diabetes), kidney and thyroid function, and — when a heart attack is suspected — troponin, a protein released when heart muscle is damaged. BNP or NT-proBNP may be checked for heart failure.

Electrocardiogram (ECG / EKG)

A quick, painless test that records the heart's electrical activity using stickers on the chest, arms and legs. It can show heart rhythm problems such as AFib, signs of a current or previous heart attack, and thickened heart muscle. It takes about 5–10 minutes.

Holter and event monitors are portable ECGs worn for 24 hours to several weeks to catch symptoms that come and go.

Echocardiogram (echo)

An ultrasound of the heart. It shows the size and shape of the chambers, how well the heart pumps (ejection fraction) and how the valves work. It's painless and uses no radiation. A transoesophageal echo passes a small probe down the throat under sedation for more detailed images.

Stress tests

These show how your heart works when it's working harder — on a treadmill or exercise bike, or with medicine if you can't exercise. They may be combined with an ECG, echo or nuclear imaging. Wear comfortable shoes and ask whether to pause any medicines beforehand.

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) score

A quick, low-dose CT scan that measures calcium in the coronary arteries, a marker of plaque. It can help some people and their doctors decide on preventive treatment such as statins.

CT coronary angiography (CTCA)

A CT scan with contrast dye that shows the coronary arteries in detail, useful for assessing chest pain. It involves an IV line and some radiation.

Invasive coronary angiography (cardiac catheterisation)

A thin tube (catheter) is passed through an artery in the wrist or groin to the heart, and dye is injected to show blockages on X-ray. If a significant blockage is found, it may be treated at the same time with angioplasty and a stent. You are usually awake but sedated.

Cardiac MRI

Detailed images of heart muscle, useful for cardiomyopathy, inflammation (myocarditis), congenital heart disease and scar tissue. No radiation, but you lie in a scanner for 30–60 minutes.

Questions to ask before any test

  1. Why do I need this test, and what will it change about my care?
  2. How do I prepare? Should I stop eating, drinking or any medicines?
  3. What are the risks?
  4. When and how will I get the results?
  5. What's the cost, and is it covered by my insurance or scheme?
