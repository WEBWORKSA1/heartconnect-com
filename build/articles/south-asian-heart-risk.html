People with roots in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Nepal — whether living in South Asia or abroad — tend to develop coronary artery disease at a younger age and often with more severe disease than many other groups. The reasons are a mix of biology, lifestyle and gaps in screening. The encouraging part is that most of the risk can be measured and reduced.

Why is the risk higher?

  • Central (abdominal) fat: South Asians tend to store more fat around the waist and organs, even at a "normal" body weight. This visceral fat is strongly linked to insulin resistance.
  • Diabetes and prediabetes: type 2 diabetes is common and often appears earlier and at lower body weights.
  • Cholesterol pattern: a tendency towards lower HDL, higher triglycerides and smaller, denser LDL particles. Elevated lipoprotein(a), an inherited risk factor, is also relatively common.
  • Diet: high intake of refined carbohydrates, fried snacks, sweets and salt in many modern diets.
  • Low physical activity and long hours of sitting.
  • Tobacco: including smokeless tobacco and bidis.

Lower thresholds matter

Because risk rises at lower body weights, many health bodies suggest lower BMI action points for Asian populations: a BMI of 23 or more may indicate increased risk and 27.5 or more high risk, compared with 25 and 30 used for many other populations. Waist size is equally important. Our BMI calculator includes an Asian cut-off option, and the waist-to-height ratio tool is a simple check: keep your waist below half your height.

If a parent or sibling had heart disease at a young age (men under 55, women under 65), tell your doctor — you may need earlier and more detailed assessment. Learn about heart tests.

Prevention that works

  1. Rebalance the plate: more vegetables, dal and legumes; fewer refined grains; swap white rice and maida for millets, brown rice and whole wheat.
  2. Watch the oil and salt: measure cooking oil, avoid vanaspati and reused frying oil, and go easy on pickles, papad and namkeen.
  3. Cut sugar: limit sweets, sweetened chai and soft drinks.
  4. Move daily: 30 minutes of brisk walking plus strength training twice a week; a short walk after meals helps blood sugar.
  5. Quit all tobacco, including chewing tobacco and gutka.
  6. Sleep and stress: aim for 7–9 hours and build in daily relaxation such as yoga or breathing practice.
